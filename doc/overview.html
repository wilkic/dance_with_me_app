Dance With Me

A phone app that removes you from your own room and puts a glowing stick figure in your place. Dance alone, or fill the room with real people and AI dancers built from real recordings.

What it does

The app never shows the camera feed. It photographs your empty room once, then uses the live camera only to find your joints. What you see is that photo with a stick figure moving where you are. That makes "removing" you perfect and free, with no segmentation and no 3D scan.

1. Pick room

"Start a room" opens your own space. "Join a room" is the phone's own picker (a spinning wheel on iOS) listing open rooms, or a grey "No rooms yet". Below the buttons is a "DJs: book a room →" link, then a dividing line, then Upcoming rooms: DJ sets booked at /schedule. The start screen says nothing about the server while it's online; it only shows a line when the server can't be reached.

2. Capture the room

Prop the phone up facing your dance space, step out of frame, tap the button. After a 3-second countdown the app averages several frames into a clean plate.

3. Dance

Step back in. Your stick figure appears. With music playing (your room's, or your own: ♫ Play music) the HUD shows its tempo, ♫ 128 bpm; there is no tempo from your dancing. Close to the camera (a DJ at the decks, someone at a laptop) the hips are out of frame, so the figure is drawn from the shoulders: head, arms, and a spine running off the bottom (bodyAnchors in stickFigure.js, issue #18). With no shoulders in view, legs only, nothing is drawn. The slider at the bottom sets how many guests (0–3) dance behind you at 90% opacity. Tapping anywhere else on the screen (or ☰) opens the menu, which changes time between guests, opacity, AI variation and where the tempo comes from on the fly.

Everything the app sends is the skeleton: 33 joints per frame. Camera video and microphone audio leave the phone only if the dancer turns those dials up.

Architecture

One data shape runs through everything: the PoseFrame, 33 MediaPipe keypoints with x, y, z and visibility, plus a timestamp. Drawing, tempo analysis, recording and the network all read it, so a remote dancer, an AI dancer and you look identical to the renderer.

Traffic goes through the server as a star, about 16 KB/s per dancer. That is what lets the server form opinions on everyone. WebRTC could replace it if latency ever matters more.

Guests: music decides who

Whoever else is in your space arrives through the same PoseChannel.subscribe path and is drawn as one more stick figure behind you. The dancer only chooses how many: one slider on the dance screen, 0–3 seats (default 1), real guests included. planGuests() in main.js decides who sits where:

  • Room-mates sit first. Everyone in your room whose movement is arriving is shown, and each one uses a seat.
  • The rest phase in. The other seats open one per "time between guests" (default 15 s): seat 1 after 15 s, seat 2 after 30 s, seat 3 after 45 s. Nobody appears the moment you start.
  • No music → delayed-yous. You, replayed on a delay (DelayedLoopbackTransport, on the phone): seat N replays you from N × 15 s ago, so it appears on time by itself. Works without a server.
  • Music → AI dancers, retimed to the music's BPM and as busy as you. Music is the room's (the host's or an OBS stream), or alone, your own: ♫ Play music works without sharing a room (file, mic or line in, tab sound), from the same button above the Guests bar. Alone it stays on your phone; only its beat, as numbers, goes to the server.
  • No strangers. Other people online no longer drop into your space (removed 22 Oct 2026, issue #23): grouping similar dancers among many is a later problem.

Keeping you quick

The pose engine (11.2 MB) and model (5.8 MB) start downloading when the start page opens, so they're usually ready by the time you tap Start. After the first visit they come from the phone's Cache Storage, with no network at all (src/pose/poseDetector.js).

Your own figure is what has to feel immediate. Guests matter less, so they are drawn the cheap way. Measured with bench.html (desktop Chrome, 1280×720; open it on a phone under npm run dev for real numbers):

Dance rooms

A room is just a name. The first person in creates it, the last out closes it. Every frame a member sends goes to the other members. Your space becomes a room when you tap "Share your room" (top left while dancing): the app picks a name no open room is using, like funky-otter and the button turns into it. Others then find it under "Join a room" on the start screen, with its dancer count. Tapping the room name opens a scrollable list of the open rooms (with their dancers and music BPM) to switch to without leaving the dance screen, plus "Exit to main menu" (issue #25). Room links (?room=) are gone. The server publishes each member's tempo and a confidence-weighted room tempo every two seconds. That number is where a future "room picks the music" feature will start.

DJ schedule (issue #2)

DJs book a room ahead at dance.wilkinscoffee.be/schedule with a DJ name, a date and start time in their own time zone, a length (30 min to 4 h), and optional set info, a bio and a link. The room is the DJ name run through the usual room-name rules, so "DJ Funk" plays in dj-funk. Every request waits for approval before it shows anywhere public. There are no accounts. Each request comes back with a secret token that stays in that browser, so "Your bookings" shows it as Waiting for approval, Approved or Not approved and offers Cancel. Requests waiting for approval don't hold the room, so a prank request can't block a real DJ. Clashes with approved sets (same room, overlapping times) are checked when a DJ asks and again when you approve. Two different rooms can overlap.

Approving: run server/deploy/deploy.sh admin to print the private link …/schedule/#admin=KEY. Opening it once stores the key in that browser and removes it from the address. Because the key sits after the #, GitHub Pages never sees it, and it only goes over the WebSocket to the dance server. The page then shows a Waiting for approval box with the full details of each request and Approve and Reject buttons, which update live. The server makes the key on first start and saves it in server/data/admin-key, or reads it from ADMIN_KEY. A connection gets 5 wrong tries and is then ignored.

Bookings live on the dance server in server/data/schedule.json. The deploy script leaves server/data/ alone, and ended sets are dropped on the next scan. Every phone gets the approved list on connect and whenever it changes. On the start screen, tapping a set shows its info, bio and link. From 10 minutes before the start until the end, the set has a Join button and appears in "Join a room" as dj-funk · DJ set, even before anyone is in it. Limits: 500 approved sets, 100 requests waiting, 10 requests per connection, at most 90 days ahead.

Fine print appears at the bottom of the start screen, on /schedule, and in the room-music picker: "Please don't share music you don't own. wilkinscoffee is not liable for your public broadcasting of unlicensed copyrighted material. We're here to dance to music, not steal it. To report a room, email Chris@wilkinscoffee.be."

Room music (issue #4)

The room's host plays music to everyone else in the room. That's the only difference between sharing a room and joining one. The host is whoever tapped "Share your room". The server recognises them by a key their browser made (hostKey, kept in localStorage; per tab until issue #20), so they get the role back after a reconnect or reload. The same key is the OBS Bearer Token for a room shared without a booking, so OBS is set up once per browser, whatever the room is called. Two tabs of one browser each sharing a room would share the key; OBS then plays to the first. During a booked set the host is the DJ instead: the browser that holds the booking's token, which /schedule saved. The host sees ♫ Play music above the Guests slider (and the same button, "♫ Play music to the room", in the ☰ menu), which opens a picker:

  • A file on this device: one or more files, played in a loop. The host hears it too.
  • Mic or line in: echo cancellation, noise suppression and auto-gain are off, so music isn't treated as speech.
  • Tab or computer sound: shown only on desktop browsers. Chrome or Edge can share a tab's sound ("Also share tab audio"), and on Windows or ChromeOS the whole system's sound. Phone browsers can't capture other apps' sound at all; only a native app could.

The sound goes straight from the host to each member over WebRTC, with Opus tuned for music: stereo, 128 kb/s, silence suppression off. The dance server only relays the signalling (rtc messages), and only between a room's host and its members. The host makes one connection per member and retries a failed one up to 3 times. Every phone playing the music, the host's and each member's, also measures its beat and sends it as numbers, so the room's tempo is the music's (see Tempo). Members see "♫ <what's playing>" in the HUD, plus "Tap to hear the music" if the browser blocks autoplay. Connections are found through Google's public STUN server only, with no TURN relay. Phones on the same wifi or ordinary home networks connect, but some mobile-data networks (carrier-grade NAT) may not. A TURN server on the VM (coturn) would fix that if it turns out to matter.

How an AI dancer is made

No choreography is generated. An AI dancer is a real person's recorded dance, retimed to your beat, with moves borrowed from another dancer of similar tempo and style.

  1. Pick a base clipTempo first, then energy, style, tag. Energy is how busy a dancer looks: mean limb speed in torso lengths per second (each clip rated as the AI dancer shows it, clipEnergy; you from your last 8 s, energyOf). Clips that match yours at natural speed win; until you've moved, it aims for a calm 0.8. Web dancers move about 3× more than people dancing at home, which made AI dancers look frantic (issue #17). It skips clips already on your floor and needs none of these to be known.
  2. Move into body spacePelvis-centred, measured in torso lengths, locomotion and camera pans removed. A landscape clip from far away lands at the same size on a portrait phone.
  3. RetimePlayback rate between 0.5× and 1.6× so the clip's tempo lands on yours. Half and double time count as the same groove, and the one that brings the clip's energy closer to yours is taken.
  4. Borrow movesFor a phrase at a time, arms or legs come from a second clip and hang off the base dancer's own shoulders or hips. Limbs swing a little further. The AI variation slider sets how far.
  5. Smooth and placeHips at 58% of the frame height, torso about 17% of it. Joint jumps are clamped and the output is smoothed because web footage jitters.
  6. StreamAbout 30 fps to your phone as ordinary PoseFrames. The beat comes in as a number, so it works for a silent dancer. Music makes the number better.

Tempo is the music's

The app never estimates tempo from dancing any more (22 Oct 2026, issue #23): it was unreliable, people opening the app aren't dancing yet, and nothing needs it. Tempo comes only from music, and is only ever shown as BPM; we can't know what song is playing, so no song titles appear anywhere.

  1. Music → numbersEvery phone playing music measures its beat with OnsetEnvelope (src/audio/onsetEnvelope.js): spectral flux at 30 Hz, sent once a second as a music message. Never the sound. In a room that's the host's phone and every listener's; alone, the music you play for yourself.
  2. Numbers → tempoThe server keeps an EnvelopeTempo per phone (8 s window, autocorrelation over 60–200 BPM, server/tempo.js) and counts a phone for 4 s after its last batch.
  3. Whose tempoIn a room with music, the clearest reading among its members; alone, your own (musicBeat in server/index.js). Sent to each dancer as tempo, to the room as room.bpm, to the start screen's room list ("funky-otter · 2 dancing · 128 BPM") and the dance floor.
  4. Tempo → AI dancersClips are picked tempo first and played 0.5–1.6× so their tempo lands on the music's (half and double time are one groove, folded into the 90–180 octave). Until the tempo is heard, a calm clip plays at its own speed.

Known gap: an OBS stream's tempo needs a phone in the room to play it. The host doesn't play their own stream, and a beat meter on a stream nobody plays out loud reads silence in Chrome.

BeatDetector (tempo from movement) stays only to rate library clips offline: a clip without a metronome label or recorded music tempo gets its tempo from the dancer's motion, which is what retiming to the music needs. The dance lab still uses it for research.

The dance library

The library is what AI dancers are made from. It indexes three folders of keypoint recordings and caches a descriptor for each clip in library/index.json, so restarts are instant. Frames load only when a clip is actually danced.

SourceWhat it holds
recordings_web/Clips harvested from web videos across 30 dance genres. Keypoints only, the video is deleted after pose extraction. Each is tagged with URL, title and genre.
recordings/Metronome sessions from the lab, labelled with a known BPM.
library/sessions/Sessions recorded with RECORD_DIR set (your own lab sessions; off on the public server, see privacy). Sessions under 300 frames are dropped.

A clip's descriptor holds its tempo, a six-number style vector, pose coverage and the source's aspect ratio. Tempo prefers the metronome label, then the music tempo, then the motion tempo. The style vector says how far arms, legs, hips and head travel and how fast arms and legs move, all in torso lengths. That is enough to tell a hip-led groove from arm-led popping without any music.

What the phone shares (privacy)

Nothing a dancer does is kept. The public promise is the page at /privacy/ (privacy/index.html, linked from the start-screen footer); keep it true whenever data handling changes. Since 18 Oct 2026 (issues #5–#9):

  • No server-side recording by default. The server keeps a session (frames, music tempo and beat) in library/sessions/ only when RECORD_DIR is set. That is for your own lab sessions; the VM leaves it unset. Uploading camera video or mic audio is gone from the app.
  • Music's beat, not its sound. Each phone playing music (the room's, or its own) sends a 30 Hz onset envelope; the server uses it live for the tempo and keeps nothing. The old ?beat=1 ambient-mic mode is gone; "♫ Play music → Mic or line in" covers it. "Teach AI" is marked coming soon. Training AI dancers on people's dances comes back later as an explicit opt-in, and the privacy page has to change first.
  • Logs: the dance server logs random session ids, never IPs. Caddy has no access log. deploy.sh caps the VM's journal at 3 days and stops copying it into /var/log/syslog.
  • DJ bookings are deleted when the set ends (Schedule.prune). Rejected and still-pending requests are deleted once their time has passed. The booking form says so right under it: "Deleted after your set · Privacy", linking to /privacy/#dj-bookings.
  • Third parties named on the page: GitHub Pages (website), Oracle Cloud Frankfurt (dance server), Google STUN (room music). Room music is peer-to-peer, so the phones in it see each other's IP.
  • Reports: the music fine print ends "To report a room, email Chris@wilkinscoffee.be." The admin view of /schedule has Live rooms with Stop music (off until the room closes) and End room (everyone solo; the name stays closed for an hour), so a report can be acted on (issue #10).
  • Visit stats (issue #11): daily totals on the dance server (server/stats.js → server/data/stats.json, 90 days, Brussels days). Each page load sends one visit (app or schedule, referring host, phone or not); the server also counts first poses (danced), rooms shared and joined, music started, bookings and peak connections. No cookies, IPs or ids. Shown in the admin view of /schedule (Visits) and by deploy.sh stats; described on the privacy page.
  • Terms of use at /terms/ (terms/index.html), linked from the footer, /schedule and the privacy page: rules, reports and moderation (DSA basics), safety, no warranty, Belgian law, legal notice, and credits. MediaPipe is Apache 2.0; its license text ships at /licenses/apache-2.0.txt.

DJ tools

Everything a DJ needs is on /dj/ (dj/index.html), linked from the music sheet and /schedule:

  • OBS overlay (src/floor.js): floor.html?room=NAME&overlay=1 draws one room's dancers in a row on a transparent canvas with a caption (room · crowd · tempo; &caption=0 hides it), no bar, no sound. Pasted into OBS as a Browser Source. /schedule offers "Copy OBS overlay link" on approved bookings.
  • Sound input (listSoundInputs in src/net/roomMusic.js): on a computer with several inputs, "Mic or line in" shows a picker, so a DJ controller's sound card, a USB interface, a line-in or a virtual cable (VB-Cable, BlackHole) carries the mix. The choice is remembered (dwm.musicInput).
  • OBS streaming (server/whip.js, library werift): OBS sends its mix over WHIP to POST /whip on the dance server (same port as the WebSocket; Caddy routes /whip*). The key is a booking token (from an hour before the set) or the host key of a room shared from a computer, as long as no booked DJ has the room. The server is ICE-lite with UDP ports WHIP_PORTS (default 50000-50999) and names WHIP_PUBLIC_IP in its answer; it takes the Opus audio, marks the video inactive, and forwards the packets to one connection per listener (everyone in the room but the host). Phones see music: { label: 'Live from OBS', live: true } and take the offer from live:<room> with the same listener code. The host's phone can only stop it; admins' Stop music / End room end it too; a stream with no audio for 20 s is dropped; OBS reconnecting replaces its old stream. The UDP range is open in both the VM's iptables and the Oracle VCN security list; a live check on 2026-10-20 (stand-in OBS from home → server → a listener) passed. The DJ guide prints the address, https://ws.wilkinscoffee.be/whip.

Deploy: deploy.sh opens the UDP range in iptables, writes a systemd drop-in with the public IP, and rewrites the Caddyfile. Oracle's security list must allow the same UDP range (done once in the console).

Wire protocol

One WebSocket per phone. Binary messages carry poses, text messages carry JSON. From the phone, a binary message is one PoseFrame. From the server, a binary message is a small envelope: one byte of id length, the sender id, then the PoseFrame.

The dance lab

A separate research toolkit, used to measure how well tempo detection really works. It was built after the first real dance showed only 80–150 BPM arm-waving ever registered, while the dancer felt a slower layer underneath.

  • Dance unit tests. Synthetic dancers with independent oscillators per body part. Six scenarios, each PASS, FAIL, or KNOWN GAP for shortcomings we mean to fix.
  • Waterfall reports. Moving-window FFT heatmaps of time against BPM, per body part, in one self-contained HTML file.
  • Real recordings. Dance to the built-in metronome while the server records, then analyse the file. Drop a recording in a folder and a sweep renders a replay video and a report.
  • Web harvesting. lab/harvest.js round-robins 30 genre searches, extracts keypoints with MediaPipe, deletes the video, and builds the library.

Measured gaps: the fastest limb wins over the groove underneath, slow sway reads one octave up because speed peaks twice per cycle, and pose jitter destabilises the lock. Web footage adds locomotion and camera moves that swamp raw coordinates, which points to pelvis-relative analysis.

Run it

# the app, on your LAN (HTTPS, accept the self-signed cert once per phone)
npm install
npm run dev

# the dance server (ws://0.0.0.0:8901)
cd server && npm install && npm start

# both at once, with a phone QR code and the big-screen dance floor
./dance-lan.sh            # --no-browser skips the floor

# tests and lab
cd server && npm test        # 57 checks: rooms, library, AI dancer, recorder, end-to-end
cd server && npm run lab     # scenario suite → lab/out/report.html

Without a server the app still works offline, solo, with delayed-you guests. The site on GitHub Pages builds from the release branch (publish with git push origin main:release; server/deploy/deploy.sh ships the same branch to the server VM) and is fully self-contained: the pose model and WebAssembly files are vendored.
